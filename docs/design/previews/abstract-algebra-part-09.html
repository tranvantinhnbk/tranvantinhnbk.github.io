Quotient groups — making cosets into elements.

Part 8 mapped every even integer to 0 and every odd integer to 1. What if we make the entire even coset one element, and the entire odd coset another?

Illustrative Part 9 lesson preview. Its wording and interaction await author review; the public reader is unchanged.

The idea: Let H = 2ℤ, E = 0 + H (all evens), and O = 1 + H (all odds). The quotient ℤ/H has just two elements: E and O. Each element is a whole set of integers.

Why can we change representatives?

If a and a′ name the same coset, then a − a′ is even. The same holds for b and b′. Therefore (a + b) − (a′ + b′) is even too. Both sums name the same result coset.

And why “normal” in other groups?

In a noncommutative group, an arbitrary subgroup may not let us combine cosets independently of the representatives. A normal subgroup is the condition that makes the quotient operation well defined. The draft lesson gives the general argument after this integer example.
