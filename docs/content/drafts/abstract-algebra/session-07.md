# Part 7: Lagrange’s theorem — counting with cosets

In Part 6, we saw that the left cosets of a subgroup cover a group without overlapping, and that each coset has the same number of elements as the subgroup. If the parent group is finite, what does this tell us about its size?

**By the end:** prove Lagrange’s theorem from the coset facts, use it to constrain subgroup and element orders, and explain why a divisor of $|G|$ need not automatically give a subgroup.

## Count the square rotations first

Let $G=\{e,r,r^2,r^3\}$ be the group of square rotations under composition, and let $H=\{e,r^2\}$.

Part 6 gave us exactly two distinct left cosets:

$$
eH=\{e,r^2\},\qquad rH=\{r,r^3\}.
$$

Each contains $2$ elements. They do not overlap, and together they contain all $4$ elements of $G$. So

$$
|G|=2\text{ cosets}\times 2\text{ elements per coset}=4.
$$

The same counting argument works for any finite group and any subgroup.

## The theorem and the meaning of index

**Theorem — Lagrange.** Let $G$ be a finite group and $H\le G$. Then $|H|$ divides $|G|$. More precisely,

$$
|G|=[G:H]\,|H|,
$$

where $[G:H]$ is the number of distinct left cosets of $H$ in $G$, called the **index** of $H$ in $G$.

For the square rotations above, $|G|=4$, $|H|=2$, and $[G:H]=2$. The brackets $[G:H]$ count cosets; they are not division notation, though the theorem shows that for a finite group $[G:H]=|G|/|H|$.

## Proof: three facts about cosets

We can prove the theorem using the facts established in Part 6. Here they are with the reasoning that makes the count work.

1. **The cosets cover $G$.** For every $a\in G$, the identity $e$ belongs to $H$, so $a=a\ast e\in aH$.
2. **Distinct cosets do not overlap.** If $aH$ and $bH$ share an element, Part 6's coset equality argument gives $aH=bH$. Thus two different cosets have no element in common.
3. **Every coset has exactly $|H|$ elements.** The map $f_a:H\to aH$ given by $f_a(h)=a\ast h$ reaches every element of $aH$ by its definition. It is one-to-one: if $a\ast h_1=a\ast h_2$, left cancellation gives $h_1=h_2$. Hence $f_a$ is a bijection.

Let $k=[G:H]$ be the number of distinct cosets. Because $G$ is finite, there are finitely many of them. Facts 1 and 2 let us count every element of $G$ exactly once; fact 3 says that each of the $k$ pieces has $|H|$ elements. Therefore

$$
|G|=k|H|=[G:H]|H|.
$$

Since $k$ is an integer, $|H|$ divides $|G|$. $\square$

The finite-group assumption matters for this divisibility statement. Part 6 also had the infinite group $(\mathbb Z,+)$ and subgroup $2\mathbb Z$, but there is no finite number $|\mathbb Z|$ to divide in the elementary counting sense used here.

## Consequence: an element's order divides the group size

Recall from Part 5 that the subgroup generated by $a\in G$ is $\langle a\rangle$, and for a finite-order element,

$$
|\langle a\rangle|=\operatorname{ord}(a).
$$

If $G$ is finite, then $\langle a\rangle\le G$ is also finite. Apply Lagrange’s theorem with $H=\langle a\rangle$:

$$
\operatorname{ord}(a)=|\langle a\rangle|\text{ divides }|G|.
$$

For square rotations, $|G|=4$. The identity has order $1$, a half-turn has order $2$, and a quarter-turn has order $4$; each divides $4$. In a group of $12$ elements, an element could only have order among $1,2,3,4,6,12$. The theorem gives a **necessary** condition, not a promise that all those orders occur in every group of size $12$.

A useful next step follows. If $a$ has order $d$, then $a^d=e$. Because $d$ divides $|G|$, write $|G|=kd$. Then

$$
a^{|G|}=a^{kd}=(a^d)^k=e.
$$

This holds for every element of a finite group. It does not say that $|G|$ is the *smallest* positive exponent returning every particular element to $e$.

## What Lagrange's theorem does not say

If a number $d$ divides $|G|$, Lagrange’s theorem does **not** by itself guarantee that $G$ has a subgroup of size $d$. The theorem starts with an existing subgroup and constrains its size; it does not construct subgroups from divisors.

For instance, knowing only that $2$ divides $12$ tells us that a size-$2$ subgroup is *allowed* by the theorem, not that this theorem alone proves one exists. We would need a separate argument to establish existence for a particular group.

Suppose $G$ is a finite group with $18$ elements and $H\le G$ has $6$ elements.

1. How many distinct left cosets of $H$ are there? Explain using the theorem.
2. Could $G$ have a subgroup with $5$ elements? Why or why not?
